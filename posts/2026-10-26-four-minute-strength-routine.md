# Just 4 Minutes a Day: The Strength Routine a New Trial Actually Tested

"I don't have time to work out" is the most common reason people give for skipping strength training. A new Penn State College of Medicine trial just tested the most extreme possible answer to that excuse: a strength routine that takes exactly **four minutes a day** — and it produced real, measurable results.

## The study

Researchers led by Christopher Sciamanna randomized **97 sedentary adults aged 65 and older** (average age 74) into two groups: one followed a four-minute daily routine, the other received no intervention for 12 weeks. The results, published in *PLOS One*, showed the exercise group improved meaningfully more than the control group on every measure tracked.

## The routine: FAST-2

The protocol, called FAST-2, is almost absurdly simple: **four exercises, 30 seconds each, with 30 seconds of rest between them** — push-ups, chair stands, two-arm resistance band rows, and stair-stepping. Add it up and the entire session runs about four minutes, done once a day.

## What actually improved after 12 weeks

Compared to the control group, the exercise group showed:

- **+4.2 chair stands** in a timed test — a direct measure of leg strength and the ability to get up from a seated position unaided
- **2.3 seconds faster** on the 5-times sit-to-stand test, a standard clinical marker of functional lower-body strength
- **+3.6 seconds** on single-leg stance time — a meaningful balance improvement, directly tied to fall risk

These aren't abstract fitness-test numbers. Chair-stand ability and single-leg balance are two of the strongest predictors of independent mobility and fall risk in older adults — the kind of measures that translate directly into real-world function, not just gym performance.

## The real finding might be the adherence rate

The strength gains are good news, but the number that should get more attention is this: participants completed the routine on **80% of possible days** over the full 12 weeks. Most exercise interventions don't fail because the exercise doesn't work — they fail because people stop doing it. A four-minute commitment turned out to be short enough that people actually kept showing up for it, week after week.

## The honest caveats

This was a small trial, and the authors themselves note that larger, longer-term efficacy studies are needed to confirm the results hold up with gold-standard assessments and over a longer period. It's also worth noting some coverage describes participants as "sedentary" while the published paper describes a population with mobility disability — worth keeping in mind when thinking about how directly this generalizes to a fully healthy, younger population.

## Why this matters beyond age 65

The headline age group is older adults, but the underlying principle applies far more broadly. The biggest obstacle to consistency for most time-crunched adults — or parents juggling a child's cricket schedule with their own fitness — isn't knowing *what* to do. It's finding a version of "enough" that's small enough to actually stick to. This study is a clean demonstration that a genuinely tiny, consistent habit can outperform the bigger program that never happens.

## Three practical takeaways

1. **Use a short, non-negotiable minimum as the floor, not the goal.** A 4-minute version of a workout that always gets done beats a 40-minute version that gets skipped three days out of five.

2. **Prioritize functional movements over volume.** The FAST-2 routine's exercises — push, stand, pull, step — map directly onto everyday movement, which is likely why the functional test results moved so clearly.

3. **Treat adherence as the actual metric that matters.** An 80% completion rate over 12 weeks is a genuinely strong result in exercise research — the "best" program is the one a person will actually do.

## The bottom line

Four minutes a day sounds too small to matter, and the old assumption was that it probably is. This trial suggests otherwise, at least for building a consistent habit and real functional strength in people starting from a low baseline. It's not a replacement for a full training program — but as a floor that keeps someone in the game, it's a genuinely useful data point.
