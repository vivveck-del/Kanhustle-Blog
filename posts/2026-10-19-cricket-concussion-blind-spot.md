# Cricket's Concussion Blind Spot: What Steve Smith's Scare Reveals About a Bigger Problem

Two cricket head-injury stories broke within the same fortnight this October. Steve Smith was struck on the helmet by an Anrich Nortje bouncer during the first Test against South Africa and has since spoken publicly about the anxiety of waiting through concussion assessment. Days earlier, Sheffield Shield opener Jake Weatherald hit his head on the boundary fence, got up looking fine, and only showed concussion symptoms later — forcing a concussion substitute mid-match. Both cases point at the same uncomfortable truth: cricket has a concussion awareness problem, and it's bigger at club level than at international level.

## The data behind the headlines

A 2026 survey of 467 Australian community cricketers found that **36% had been struck on the head, helmet, or neck at least once in the past 12 months**, and roughly **35% of those hit reported an actual traumatic head injury** — not a near-miss, a real one. That's a meaningfully high rate of head contact in a sport most parents don't associate with collision risk the way they would rugby or boxing.

The bigger problem isn't the hits — it's what players and coaches know about what to do afterward. Around **80% of surveyed players were familiar with helmet safety advice**, but only **49% knew the actual concussion and head-trauma guidelines**. Knowing you should wear a helmet and knowing how to handle a possible concussion are two completely different kinds of knowledge, and cricket culture has only really taught the first one.

## Why Weatherald's case matters more than Smith's

Smith's situation — struck, monitored, publicly cautious about returning — is cricket's concussion protocol working close to as intended, even if uncomfortable. Weatherald's is the more instructive case for club and age-group cricket: **he looked fine immediately after impact**, and the concussion only became apparent later in the match. That delayed-onset pattern is exactly what the research warns about, and it's exactly what gets missed when a coach's standard is "he got up walking, he's fine."

## What the actual protocol requires

The ICC's concussion management guidelines are specific, and worth knowing beyond just "see a doctor if it's bad":

- Any player showing concussion symptoms **must leave the match immediately** — there's no playing through it to see how it goes.
- Reassessment happens with a standardized tool (SCAT6) at the end of play, and **again one to two days later**, specifically because symptoms can emerge or evolve after the initial check.
- **Formal medical clearance is required before any return to training or play — and it can never happen on the day of the injury itself**, regardless of how fine the player looks or feels hours later.

That last point directly addresses the Weatherald scenario: even an athlete who seems unaffected immediately after a blow to the head is not cleared to continue that same day under the actual guidelines.

## Three things worth changing at club and academy level

1. **Treat every head/helmet impact as a concussion check, not just the ones that look bad.** The data on delayed symptoms means "they got up fine" isn't a valid clearance — it's the start of an observation window, not the end of one.

2. **Close the 80%-vs-49% gap deliberately.** Helmet use is already well understood; concussion protocol isn't. That's a specific, fixable education gap, not a vague awareness problem.

3. **Make same-day return a hard no, every time.** The ICC guideline is unambiguous on this point, and it's the single easiest rule to enforce consistently even without medical staff on-site.

## The bottom line

Smith's and Weatherald's cases, two weeks apart, aren't freak incidents — they're a reminder that head contact in cricket is common enough that every club needs an actual concussion protocol, not just a helmet policy. The gap isn't in equipment. It's in knowing what to do in the hour and days after impact, especially when the player insists they feel fine.
