# Want More Bat Speed? Here's What the Research Says Actually Builds It

With the 2026–27 domestic season opening on October 11, this is exactly the window when batters are chasing extra power before the overs start piling up — usually by adding more throwdowns and net sessions. But a study on elite cricketers published in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* found something batters rarely train deliberately: the physical qualities that actually predict bat speed largely come from outside the nets, not from more reps inside them.

## What the research actually measured

Researchers tested a battery of physical qualities against measured bat speed in elite cricketers and found three stood out as critical: **absolute upper-body pulling strength, dominant-side rotational power, and total-body isometric strength**. Net time builds timing, shot selection, and feel — but it doesn't reliably build the underlying strength and power that generates raw swing speed. Those are separate, trainable qualities that need their own dedicated work.

## The mechanics behind the number

The biomechanics explain why these specific qualities matter. Batters who can rotate their shoulders further than their hips before the downswing create stored rotational tension in the torso — often called the "X-factor stretch" — which releases as whip-like rotational speed through the bat. Two additional technical elements amplify this: straightening the lead arm during the downswing generates more bat speed than keeping it flexed, and delaying the wrist "uncocking" (release) until the last possible moment adds a final multiplying effect right before contact.

The key point: none of these technical elements work in isolation. Good technique without the underlying pulling strength and rotational power to fuel it caps out early — the same way a good bowling action without conditioning breaks down under repeated load. Technique and physical capacity have to be built together.

## What about training with a heavier bat?

Overload/underload bat training — deliberately practicing swings with a bat heavier or lighter than your match bat — does work, but only within a specific range. Research shows consistent swing-speed gains after 4–6 weeks of overload/underload work when the bat weight stays within **±10–15%** of match weight. Push much beyond a 20% difference in either direction, and it starts actively hurting timing and technique instead of helping — the classic case of more not being better.

## Training should match your format

The research is also clear that power training isn't one-size-fits-all across formats. For batters whose primary game is T20, the priority should be power, speed, repeat-sprint ability, and rotational strength, with just enough aerobic conditioning to recover between overs — not the other way around. A batter training like a Test-match all-rounder when their actual game is built around boundary-hitting in a 20-over format is training the wrong energy system.

## Four practical additions to a batter's training week

1. **Add dedicated pulling-strength work.** Rows, pull-ups, and pulling-focused resistance training build the upper-body strength the research links directly to bat speed — not just general gym strength, but specifically pulling patterns.

2. **Train rotational power separately from technique.** Cable rotations and rotational medicine ball throws build the "X-factor" hip-shoulder separation that technique drills alone don't develop.

3. **Use overload/underload bats correctly.** Stay within roughly ±10–15% of match bat weight, in 4–6 week blocks — and don't assume a heavier bat automatically means more power.

4. **Match your conditioning to your format.** T20-focused batters should weight training toward power and repeat-sprint capacity; longer-format batters need a broader aerobic and strength-endurance base.

## The bottom line

More net sessions alone won't build meaningfully more bat speed — the research says the ceiling is set by pulling strength, rotational power, and total-body strength trained deliberately away from the nets.
